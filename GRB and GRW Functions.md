## Abstract

This paper defines two delay functions derived from the Gaussian distribution and intended for retry scheduling. The first is a **monotone, bounded backoff curve** obtained from the Gaussian cumulative distribution function (CDF); it rises smoothly from an initial delay and saturates at an implementor-defined cap. The second is a **localized Gaussian window** obtained from the probability density function (PDF); it produces a deliberate hump that can be used as a standalone schedule or composed with the backoff curve.

Both functions take a 1-based retry attempt index $k$ as input and return an integer delay in milliseconds (or any preferred delay say, minutes or hours). In its base form, Branch A fixes the first retry at $D_0$, is non-decreasing, and saturates at the local scheduling cap $D_{\mathrm{cap}}$. That first-retry guarantee is **mode-specific**: the base CDF and the capped increment-multiplicative composition preserve $D(1) = D_0$; the standalone window and the additive and multiplicative compositions can raise the first retry above $D_0$. Every delay generated by the algorithm is bounded by $D_{\mathrm{cap}}$; the effective wait after a server `Retry-After` instruction may exceed it. Jitter is optional and non-reproducible per call. The paper gives the equations, parameterization rules, a language-agnostic algorithm, a C# reference implementation, six worked use cases, a reproducible synthetic comparison, and a list of implementation and operational pitfalls. Section 13 surveys the nearest prior art and states precisely how this construction differs from established practice.

The central idea is simple: **the Gaussian density is the rate at which a delay grows; the Gaussian CDF is the delay itself.** The original hump-shaped formula is therefore not discarded — it becomes the derivative of the monotone schedule.

## 1. Scope, terminology, and conventions

| Term                                         | Definition                                                                                                                                                       |
| -------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Initial attempt**                          | The first execution of the operation, before any retry. It is not assigned a retry index.                                                                        |
| **Retry attempt $k$**                        | The $k$-th retry after the initial failure. $k = 1$ is the first retry.                                                                                          |
| **Nominal delay $D(k)$**                     | The deterministic delay before retry $k$, in milliseconds.                                                                                                       |
| **Actual delay**                             | The nominal delay after optional jitter, rounded to an integer.                                                                                                  |
| **Local scheduling cap $D_{\mathrm{cap}}$**  | The maximum delay produced by the retry algorithm itself.                                                                                                        |
| **Effective retry delay $D_{\mathrm{eff}}$** | The final wait after combining the local delay with server instructions such as `Retry-After` and with any operation deadline. It may exceed $D_{\mathrm{cap}}$. |
| **Jitter**                                   | A random perturbation applied to the nominal delay to avoid synchronized retries.                                                                                |
| **Window $W(k)$**                            | A localized Gaussian contribution centered on a chosen attempt.                                                                                                  |
| **Backoff**                                  | A schedule whose delay is non-decreasing in $k$.                                                                                                                 |
| **Retry window**                             | A schedule or modifier whose delay rises and then falls around a chosen attempt band.                                                                            |

Conventions used throughout:

- $k \in  \mathbb{N}$, $k \ge  1$.
- All delays are in **milliseconds** and are returned as **integers**.
- The initial attempt is $k = 0$; the first retry is $k = 1$.
- The standard normal CDF is $\Phi$; the standard normal PDF is $\varphi$.
- The error function is `erf`.

## 2. Design goals and non-goals

### 2.1 Goals

1. **Monotone base backoff.** $D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(k + 1) \ge  D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(k)$ for all $k \ge  1$ before jitter.
2. **Mode-specific exact initial delay.** The base CDF satisfies $D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(1) = D_0$, and the capped increment-multiplicative composition preserves $D(1) = D_0$. Additive, multiplicative, and standalone-window modes do **not** guarantee this; they are documented separately in S5.4.
3. **Locally bounded, globally honest.** The algorithm generates $D_0 \le  D_{\mathrm{local}}(k) \le  D_{\mathrm{cap}}$ for every $k$. The effective delay $D_{\mathrm{eff}} = \max(D_{\mathrm{local}}, \mathrm{retryAfter}, \ldots )$ may exceed $D_{\mathrm{cap}}$ when a server or deadline explicitly requires it (S10.5).
4. **Smooth knee.** The delay should accelerate gently and flatten into the cap rather than switching abruptly.
5. **Integer output.** Every delay is an integer number of milliseconds.
6. **Optional jitter.** Jitter can be disabled entirely; when enabled it is fresh per call, not derived from a request identifier.
7. **Localized control.** A separate window function can concentrate extra delay in a chosen attempt band without changing the main backoff.
8. **Portable specification.** The algorithm must be implementable in any language with `exp`, `erf`, and a uniform random source.

### 2.2 Non-goals

- This is **not a rate limiter**. A client can ignore a delay; enforcement must happen server-side.
- This is **not a substitute for idempotency**. Only retry operations that are safe to repeat.
- This is **not a cryptographic random-number generator**. Jitter is for desynchronization, not secrecy.
- This is **not a model of server load**. It is a client-side pacing policy; server signals such as `Retry-After` take precedence.
- This is **not a circuit breaker**. It should be composed with one, not replace one.

## 3. Mathematical background

The standard normal density and distribution functions are

$$
\varphi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left(-\frac{z^2}{2}\right)
$$

$$
\Phi(z) = \int_{-\infty}^{z} \varphi(t)\,dt
= \frac{1}{2}\left[1 + \mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right]
$$

The key identity is

$$
\frac{d\Phi(z)}{dz} = \varphi(z)
$$

A Gaussian kernel with center $m$ and scale $s$ is

$$
g(x) = \exp\left(-\frac{(x-m)^2}{2s^2}\right)
$$

Its integral is

$$
\int g(x)\,dx = s\sqrt{2\pi}\,\Phi\left(\frac{x-m}{s}\right)
$$

This is the bridge between the two branches:

- The **PDF** $\varphi$ is hump-shaped and localized. It is useful as a **window** or as a **rate**.
- The **CDF** $\Phi$ is monotone and bounded. It is the correct primitive for a **delay** that must grow with the attempt index.

## 4. Branch A — Monotone Gaussian backoff

### 4.1 Requirement

A retry delay must not shrink as the number of failures grows. A raw Gaussian

$$
y(k) = a\exp\left(-\frac{(k-b)^2}{2c^2}\right)
$$

rises to a peak at $k = b$ and then **falls**. Unless $b$ is placed beyond the maximum retry count, late retries would receive shorter delays than early ones. That is the opposite of the intended behavior. The monotone member of the Gaussian family is its integral, the CDF.

### 4.2 Canonical equation

Let

- $D_0$ = initial delay (ms),
- $D_{\mathrm{cap}}$ = maximum delay (ms), with $D_{\mathrm{cap}} > D_0$,
- $A = D_{\mathrm{cap}} - D_0$ = total rise,
- $m \ge  0$ = ramp location parameter in $n = k - 1$ units (the half-cap is $m + 1 + \delta$),
- $s > 0$ = ramp scale in attempts,
- $n = k - 1$.

Then the nominal delay is

$$
D(k) = D_0 + A\,\frac{\Phi\left(\frac{n-m}{s}\right) - \Phi\left(-\frac{m}{s}\right)}{1 - \Phi\left(-\frac{m}{s}\right)}
$$

This is the normalized integral of the Gaussian kernel over the interval $[0, n]$. The denominator $1 - \Phi (-m/s)$ conditions the curve so that the first retry starts exactly at $D_0$.

### 4.3 Properties

**P1 — Exact initial delay (base CDF only).**
At $k = 1$, $n = 0$, the numerator is $\Phi (-m/s) - \Phi (-m/s) = 0$, so $D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(1) = D_0$.

This is a property of the base CDF, not of every schedule built from it. Composition can add a window contribution at $k = 1$, so the composed delay may exceed $D_0$. The mode-specific behaviour is tabulated in S5.4. Only the base CDF and the capped increment-multiplicative composition preserve the exact first retry.

**P2 — Saturation at the cap.**
As $k \to  \infty$, $\Phi ((n - m)/s) \to  1$, so the fraction tends to $(1 - \Phi (-m/s)) / (1 - \Phi (-m/s)) = 1$, and $D(k) \to  D_{\mathrm{cap}}$.

**P3 — Monotonicity.**
$\Phi$ is strictly increasing, $A > 0$, and $1 - \Phi (-m/s) > 0$. Therefore $D(k)$ is strictly increasing in $k$ before quantization, and non-decreasing after rounding.

**P4 — Local boundedness.**
$\Phi  \in  (0, 1)$, so the fraction lies in $[0, 1)$. Hence $D_0 \le  D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(k) < D_{\mathrm{cap}}$, and clamping guarantees that every delay produced by the algorithm satisfies $D_{\mathrm{local}}(k) \le  D_{\mathrm{cap}}$. Composed delay functions retain that bound only if they clamp or cap explicitly (S5.4).

The bound is on the *local scheduling cap*, not on the final wait. When a server returns `Retry-After`, the effective delay is $D_{\mathrm{eff}} = \max(D_{\mathrm{local}}, \mathrm{retryAfter}, \ldots )$ and may exceed $D_{\mathrm{cap}}$; see S1 and S10.5.

**P5 — Half-cap point.**
The delay reaches half of the range where

$$
\Phi\left(\frac{n^* - m}{s}\right) = \frac{1 + \Phi(-m/s)}{2}
$$

so

$$
n^* = m + s\,\Phi^{-1}\left(\frac{1 + \Phi(-m/s)}{2}\right)
$$

Define the offset between the half-cap attempt and $k = m + 1$ by

$$
\delta = s\,\Phi^{-1}\left(\frac{1 + \Phi(-m/s)}{2}\right) \ge 0
$$

so that

$$
n^* = m + \delta, \qquad k^* = m + 1 + \delta
$$

The offset $\delta$ is non-zero because the normalised curve starts at the lower endpoint and therefore has to make up the truncated left tail. It is small but not zero: for $m = 3$, $s = 1.5$ it is $\delta  \approx  0.0428$, so the exact half-cap attempt is $k^* \approx  4.043$. For $m \ge  2s$, $\delta  \approx  0.03 s$, which stays below 0.1 attempt for the ramp widths used here; for small $m$ it can be larger ($m = 1$, $s = 0.8$ gives $\delta  \approx  0.107$). Treat $k \approx  m + 1$ as an approximation, not as the definition of the half-cap point.

### 4.4 Derivative: the Gaussian as a rate

Treating $n$ as continuous,

$$
\frac{dD}{dn} = \frac{A\,\varphi\left(\frac{n-m}{s}\right)}{s\left[1 - \Phi(-m/s)\right]}
$$

This is the original Gaussian hump, appearing now as the **rate of increase** of the delay. Its peak is at $n = m$ and equals

$$
\frac{A}{s\sqrt{2\pi}\left[1 - \Phi(-m/s)\right]}
$$

So the density is not the delay; it is how fast the delay grows. This is the precise sense in which the original Gaussian idea survives: it is the derivative of the backoff curve.

### 4.5 Simplified asymptotic form

If the requirement $D(1) = D_0$ is relaxed, the normalization can be dropped:

$$
D(k) \approx D_0 + A\,\Phi\left(\frac{k-\mu}{\sigma}\right),
\qquad \mu = m+1,\quad \sigma = s
$$

Here $D(1) = D_0 + A \cdot  \Phi ((1 - \mu )/\sigma )$, which is slightly above $D_0$. The canonical form in S4.2 is preferred because it starts exactly at the configured initial delay.

### 4.6 Parameterization

The parameters have direct operational interpretations:

| Parameter          | Meaning                                                  | Typical value      |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------ |
| $D_0$              | Delay before the first retry                             | 500–900 ms         |
| $D_{\mathrm{cap}}$ | Maximum delay                                            | Implementor choice |
| $m$                | Ramp location in $n$ units (half-cap ≈ $m + 1 + \delta$) | 2–8                |
| $s$                | Ramp scale                                               | Ramp width / 4     |
| $A$                | Total rise, $D_{\mathrm{cap}} - D_0$                     | Derived            |

**Choosing $m$.** If the delay should be halfway to the cap at retry attempt $K_{\mathrm{half}}$, the first estimate is

$$
m_0 = K_{\mathrm{half}} - 1
$$

This is not exact: the exact half-cap attempt is $m + 1 + \delta (m)$, where $\delta$ is defined in S4.3. To hit $K_{\mathrm{half}}$ precisely, solve the fixed-point equation

$$
m = K_{\mathrm{half}} - 1 - s\,\Phi^{-1}\left(\frac{1 + \Phi(-m/s)}{2}\right)
$$

by starting at $m_0$ and iterating; the correction is small and the iteration converges in a few steps. Equivalently, use the `HalfAttemptsForTarget` helper in the reference implementation. Reporting $m = K_{\mathrm{half}} - 1$ as if it were exact overstates the precision of the parameterization.

**Choosing $s$.** If the transition should span roughly $W$ attempts, set

$$
s = \frac{W}{4}
$$

so that $m \pm  2s$ covers about 95% of the rise. This preserves the original “fourth division” intuition, now with a correct statistical meaning.

**Example.** $D_0 = 500\ \text{ms}$, $D_{\mathrm{cap}} = 8{,}000\ \text{ms}$, target half-cap at attempt 4, ramp width 6 attempts:

$$
\begin{aligned}
m &\approx 4 - 1 - 0.046 = 2.954\\
s &= 6/4 = 1.5\\
A &= 8{,}000 - 500 = 7{,}500
\end{aligned}
$$

With $m = 2.954$ the exact half-cap is 4.000. Rounding $m$ to 3 moves the exact half-cap to `4.043`, which is close enough for operational use; the sequence in Use Case 1 uses $m = 3$. If the half-cap location is a hard requirement rather than a design target, keep the solved value of $m$ (2.954) instead of rounding it.

### 4.7 Integer quantization

The nominal delay is a real number. The delivered delay is

$$
D_{\mathrm{int}}(k) = \mathrm{clamp}\left(\mathrm{round}(D(k)), D_0, D_{\mathrm{cap}}\right)
$$

with `round` using a consistent convention (for example, half away from zero). Three details matter:

- **Rounding can create plateaus.** Near the floor and cap, consecutive attempts may round to the same integer. This is acceptable for a non-decreasing schedule.
- **Strict increase is a stronger requirement.** If every retry must be strictly longer than the previous one, enforce $D_{\mathrm{int}}(k) \ge  D_{\mathrm{int}}(k - 1) + 1$ until the cap is reached. Otherwise, document that plateaus are possible.
- **Sub-millisecond delays need an explicit policy.** With $D_0 \ge  1$ the first retry is already at least one millisecond, so no special case arises. If $D_0 = 0$ is permitted, then for small $k$ the nominal delay can round to `0`. Decide between three policies: (a) forbid by requiring $D_0 \ge  1$; (b) allow `0` and treat it as "retry immediately"; (c) enforce a floor of `1` ms with $\max(1, \mathrm{round}(D(k)))$. Policy (c) changes $D(1)$ when $D_0 = 0$, so it must be documented as part of the schedule rather than applied silently in the transport layer. Negative values cannot occur after clamping, because $D_0 \ge  0$.

### 4.8 Jitter

Jitter desynchronizes clients that failed at the same time. It is optional and, in this specification, **non-reproducible per request**: it is drawn from a fresh entropy source rather than seeded from a request identifier.

Symmetric multiplicative jitter:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_j(k) &= \mathrm{clamp}\left(\mathrm{round}\left(D(k)\,(1 + j\,(2U-1))\right), D_0, D_{\mathrm{cap}}\right)\\
U &\sim \mathrm{Uniform}[0,1)\\
j &\in [0,1)
\end{aligned}
$$

A value $j = 0.2$ means the pre-clamp delay is uniformly distributed between 80% and 120% of nominal. Clamping changes the distribution at both ends, and the effect is not symmetric at the first retry or at the nominal cap.

Let $d = D(k)$, $L = d(1 - j)$, and $H = d(1 + j)$. For $j > 0$ the pre-clamp density is uniform on $[L, H]$. After clamping to $[D_0, D_{\mathrm{cap}}]$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Pr(D_j = D_0) &= \frac{\max(0, D_0 - L)}{H - L}\\
\Pr(D_j = D_{\mathrm{cap}}) &= \frac{\max(0, H - D_{\mathrm{cap}})}{H - L}
\end{aligned}
$$

and the remaining mass is uniform on $(\max(L, D_0), \min(H, D_{\mathrm{cap}}))$.

**The degenerate case $j = 0$.** Then $L = H = d$ and the ratio is $0/0$; the formulas above do not apply. Jitter is disabled and the realized delay is the deterministic value $D_j(k) = D(k)$, already inside $[D_0, D_{\mathrm{cap}}]$. Implementations should branch on $j > 0$ rather than evaluate the ratio, and should not treat a zero-width distribution as a clamp event.

Two consequences matter operationally:

- **At the first retry**, the base CDF gives $d = D_0$, so $L = D_0(1 - j) < D_0$. Half of the pre-clamp mass falls below the floor and is clipped, giving $\Pr(D_j(1) = D_0) = 1/2$ and a uniform spread on $(D_0, D_0(1 + j))$. The realized first-retry delay is therefore **not** symmetric about $D_0$; it is biased upward. If a symmetric first-retry distribution is required, start the jitter band at $k = 2$, or use a jitter policy whose support respects the floor. This bullet assumes the base CDF; in an additive or multiplicative composition with $W(1) > 0$, the first nominal delay is already above $D_0$ and the clipping fraction is correspondingly smaller.
- **At the nominal cap**, the analysis is narrower than it first appears. The CDF approaches $D_{\mathrm{cap}}$ asymptotically and does not reach it at any finite $k$ in exact arithmetic, so the "half the mass is clipped" statement applies only when the nominal input is **exactly** $D_{\mathrm{cap}}$. That happens when (a) the caller explicitly saturates the schedule at the cap, (b) integer rounding produces $D_{\mathrm{int}}(k) = D_{\mathrm{cap}}$, or (c) a composition clamps to the cap before jitter. In those cases $H = D_{\mathrm{cap}}(1 + j) > D_{\mathrm{cap}}$, so half the mass is clipped and $\Pr(D_j = D_{\mathrm{cap}}) = 1/2$. For a late but unsaturated retry the clipping fraction is $\max(0, H - D_{\mathrm{cap}})/(H - L)$, which is generally less than one half. The cap remains a hard upper bound on the realized delay either way.

Alternatives:

- **Positive-only jitter:** $D_j(k) = D(k) \cdot  (1 + j \cdot  U)$, with $U \in  [0, 1)$. This avoids short delays but still does not guarantee monotonicity across attempts.
- **Full jitter:** $D_j(k) \sim  \mathrm{Uniform}(D_0, D(k))$. More aggressive desynchronization, but can substantially shorten delays.
- **Increment jitter:** jitter each increment rather than the level; this preserves monotonicity if the jittered increment is non-negative.

Jitter necessarily weakens strict monotonicity; S4.9 separates the guarantees. If realized-delay monotonicity is a hard requirement, either disable jitter, use positive-only jitter with a monotone bound, or jitter the increments.

### 4.9 Three senses of monotonicity

Statements such as “the schedule is monotone” are ambiguous once jitter and composition are involved. This specification distinguishes:

1. **Nominal monotonicity.** The deterministic Branch A curve satisfies $D(k + 1) \ge  D(k)$ for all $k \ge  1$. This is guaranteed by S4.3 (P3) for every valid parameter set.
2. **Composed nominal monotonicity.** The deterministic schedule remains non-decreasing after a window is applied. Additive and multiplicative compositions do **not** guarantee this; the capped increment-multiplicative composition does (S5.4–S5.5).
3. **Realized-delay monotonicity.** The sequence of jittered delays observed by one client is non-decreasing. This is **not** guaranteed by independent per-attempt jitter, even when (1) and (2) hold, and it should not be claimed.

Unless a statement names one of these three, it should be read as nominal. The invariants in S11.1 are labelled accordingly.

## 5. Branch B — Gaussian retry window (hump)

### 5.1 Definition

The retry window is a localized Gaussian contribution:

$$
W(k) = A_w \exp\left(-\frac{(k-\mu_w)^2}{2\sigma_w^2}\right)
$$

where

- $A_w \ge  0$ is the peak height (ms),
- $\mu_w > 0$ is the center attempt,
- $\sigma_w > 0$ is the width in attempts.

### 5.2 Properties

**Peak.** $W(\mu_w) = A_w$, and $W(k) < A_w$ elsewhere.

**Symmetry.** $W(\mu_w - d) = W(\mu_w + d)$.

**Full width at half maximum.**

$$
\mathrm{FWHM} = 2\sigma_w\sqrt{2\ln 2} \approx 2.3548\,\sigma_w
$$

**Approximate 95% band.** $\mu_w \pm  1.96\sigma_w$; in discrete practice, $\mu_w \pm  2\sigma_w$.

**Effective support.** The window is negligible beyond $\mu_w \pm  3\sigma_w$ (about 1% of the peak).

**Continuous integral.**

$$
\int W(k)\,dk = A_w\,\sigma_w\sqrt{2\pi}
$$

**Parameterization.** To place a window across attempts $a$ through $b$, set

$$
\mu_w = \frac{a+b}{2}, \qquad \sigma_w = \frac{b-a}{4}
$$

This is the same “quarter of the width” rule used for the backoff.

### 5.3 Standalone windowed delay

Used alone, the window produces a hump:

$$
D_w(k) = \mathrm{clamp}\left(D_0 + W(k), D_0, D_{\mathrm{cap}}\right)
$$

The delay rises to $D_0 + A_w$ at $k = \mu_w$ and then falls back toward $D_0$. This is appropriate only when a deliberate slow-down followed by a speed-up is desired. It is **not** a backoff in the monotone sense.

**First retry.** A standalone window does **not** preserve $D_w(1) = D_0$ unless it is specially configured. In general $W(1) > 0$, so the first retry is delayed by $D_0 + W(1)$. If an exact first retry matters, either subtract $W(1)$ from the window, start the window at $k = 2$, or use the capped increment-multiplicative composition of S5.4.

Typical standalone uses:

- A **cooldown band** around a specific retry count, after which the client is allowed to retry more aggressively again.
- **Probe spacing** in a circuit-breaker half-open state, where probes are spread out around the middle of the probe window.
- **Load-shedding** during a known peak, where extra delay is concentrated in a narrow band of attempts.

### 5.4 Composition with the backoff

The window is usually more useful as a modifier on the monotone backoff.

**Additive composition.**

$$
D(k) = \mathrm{clamp}\left(D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(k) + W(k), D_0, D_{\mathrm{cap}}\right)
$$

**Multiplicative composition.**

$$
D(k) = \mathrm{clamp}\left(D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(k)\,(1 + w\,G(k)), D_0, D_{\mathrm{cap}}\right)
$$

$$
G(k) = \exp\left(-\frac{(k-\mu_w)^2}{2\sigma_w^2}\right) \quad (\text{peaks at } 1),
\qquad w \ge 0
$$

**Increment-multiplicative composition (canonical monotone form).**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(j) &= D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(j) - D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(j-1), \qquad D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(0) = D_0\\
D(k) &= \min\left(D_{\mathrm{cap}},\; D_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{k} \Delta D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(j)\,(1 + w\,G(j))\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

The outer `min` is essential, not cosmetic: the uncapped sum can exceed $D_{\mathrm{cap}}$, and omitting the cap produces a schedule that violates the hard upper bound. Because every increment is non-negative and $\min(D_{\mathrm{cap}}, \cdot )$ is non-decreasing, the capped form is non-decreasing even though the window rises and falls.

This is the **canonical composition** to use when composed nominal monotonicity matters. The convention $D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(0) = D_0$ makes $\Delta D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(1) = 0$, so the window has no effect on the first retry; that is intentional, since the first retry is already pinned at $D_0$.

**First-retry guarantee by mode.** The exact-first-retry property is mode-specific. The following table states the nominal, pre-jitter behaviour; jitter is a separate stage (S4.8) and can raise the realized first retry above $D_0$ even in a mode that preserves the nominal value.

| Mode                            | Nominal $D(1) = D_0$? | Reason                                                                             |
| ------------------------------- | --------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Base Gaussian CDF               | Yes                   | Normalization makes the numerator zero at $k = 1$                                  |
| Standalone Gaussian window      | No (unless adjusted)  | $W(1) > 0$ is added to $D_0$                                                       |
| Additive composition            | No (unless adjusted)  | $D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(1) = D_0$, but $W(1) > 0$                                    |
| Multiplicative composition      | No (unless adjusted)  | $D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(1) = D_0$, but the factor $1 + w\cdot G(1) > 1$              |
| Capped increment-multiplicative | Yes                   | $\Delta D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(1) = 0$, so the first increment is window-independent |

### 5.5 Monotonicity conditions for each composition

Adding a hump to a monotone curve can create a local maximum and a subsequent dip. The window’s steepest negative slope is

$$
\max |W'(k)| = \frac{A_w}{\sigma_w\sqrt{e}} \approx 0.6065\,\frac{A_w}{\sigma_w}
$$

at $k = \mu_w \pm  \sigma_w$. For **additive** composition, a sufficient condition for the composed schedule to remain non-decreasing over a finite retry horizon is

$$
\frac{A_w}{\sigma_w\sqrt{e}} \le \min_{1 \le k < K}\left[D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(k+1) - D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(k)\right]
$$

For **multiplicative** composition, with unit-height window $G$, the corresponding conservative condition is

$$
\frac{w\,D_{\mathrm{cap}}}{\sigma_w\sqrt{e}} \le \min_{1 \le k < K}\left[D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(k+1) - D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(k)\right]
$$

Here $K$ is the **final retry index under evaluation**, so the minimum runs over $k = 1, 2, \ldots , K - 1$ and the increments compared are those actually used by the schedule. The range must be finite. The CDF increments tend to zero as $k \to  \infty$, so an infimum over all $k \ge  1$ is zero and no positive window amplitude could satisfy the bound. A claimed guarantee therefore has to name its horizon: "monotone for $k \le  K$", not "monotone".

The right-hand side is smallest near the cap, where the CDF curve flattens, so the bound is deliberately conservative: it ignores the clamp and the fact that the window is negligible in its tails. Use it as a quick screen, then verify the composed sequence or use the capped increment-multiplicative form. Both bounds are sufficient, not necessary.

| Composition                          | Composed nominal monotonicity | Guarantee or condition                                                                                |
| ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Standalone window                    | No, by design                 | —                                                                                                     |
| Additive                             | Not automatic                 | $A_w / (\sigma_w\sqrt{e}) \le \min_{1 \le k < K} \Delta D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(k)$                      |
| Multiplicative                       | Not automatic                 | $w \cdot D_{\mathrm{cap}} / (\sigma_w\sqrt{e}) \le \min_{1 \le k < K} \Delta D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(k)$ |
| Increment-multiplicative, capped     | Yes                           | Non-negative increments and explicit $\min(D_{\mathrm{cap}}, \cdot )$                                 |
| Any composition + independent jitter | Realized delays not monotone  | Only bounds $[D_0, D_{\mathrm{cap}}]$ are guaranteed                                                  |

In practice, either keep the window wide and low, or use the capped increment-multiplicative form, which is monotone by construction.

## 6. Composing the two branches

A complete schedule can be assembled as:

$$
D(k) = \mathrm{clamp}\left(D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(k) + W(k), D_0, D_{\mathrm{cap}}\right) \qquad \text{(additive)}
$$

or

$$
D(k) = \mathrm{clamp}\left(D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(k)\,(1 + w\,G(k)), D_0, D_{\mathrm{cap}}\right) \qquad \text{(multiplicative)}
$$

The additive form is easiest to reason about in milliseconds. The multiplicative form preserves the relative shape of the backoff. The **capped increment-multiplicative form (S5.4) is the canonical choice when composed nominal monotonicity is non-negotiable**; it is the only composition in this specification that is non-decreasing by construction.

Recommended defaults:

| Setting            | Value                                                                 |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| $D_0$              | 500 ms (interactive), 800 ms (OTP/email)                              |
| $D_{\mathrm{cap}}$ | 8,000–30,000 ms for interactive; up to 300,000 ms for background work |
| $m$                | 3 (exact half-cap at attempt 4.043 for $s = 1.5$)                     |
| $s$                | 1.5 (6-attempt ramp)                                                  |
| $j$                | 0.2 (optional)                                                        |
| `maxAttempts`      | 5–9 interactive; 10–16 background                                     |

Reference profiles for short, moderate, and long retry horizons:

| Profile             | $D_0$ | $D_{\mathrm{cap}}$ | $m$ | $s$ | $j$ | `maxAttempts` | Exact half-cap |
| ------------------- | ----- | ------------------ | --- | --- | --- | ------------- | -------------- |
| Short / interactive | 500   | 8,000              | 3   | 1.5 | 0.2 | 9             | 4.043          |
| Moderate / service  | 800   | 30,000             | 4   | 1.5 | 0.2 | 12            | 5.007          |
| Long / batch        | 1,000 | 300,000            | 8   | 2.5 | 0.3 | 16            | 9.002          |

The half-cap column is computed from S4.3, not from $m + 1$. If a profile must hit an integer half-cap attempt exactly, solve for $m$ with `HalfAttemptsForTarget` rather than rounding $m = K_{\mathrm{half}} - 1$.

## 7. Language-agnostic algorithm

### 7.1 Preconditions

- $k \ge  1$.
- $D_{\mathrm{cap}} > D_0 \ge  0$.
- $m \ge  0$.
- $s > 0$.
- $0 \le  j < 1$.
- $A_w \ge  0$, $\mu_w > 0$, $\sigma_w > 0$, $w \ge  0$.

### 7.2 Pseudocode

```text
CONFIG:
    D0          // initial delay, ms
    Dcap        // cap, ms
    m           // ramp location in n-space; half-cap is m + 1 + delta
    s           // ramp scale in attempts
    j           // jitter fraction, 0 disables
    w           // maximum fractional boost for window compositions
    Aw, muw, sw // optional window
    maxAttempts // total attempts including the initial one

FUNCTION Phi(z):
    RETURN 0.5 * (1 + erf(z / sqrt(2)))

FUNCTION PhiInv(p):
    // Inverse standard normal CDF for 0 < p < 1.
    // Bisection is shown for portability; a library implementation is fine.
    lo = -40; hi = 40
    REPEAT 100 times:
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        IF Phi(mid) < p: lo = mid ELSE hi = mid
    RETURN (lo + hi) / 2

FUNCTION HalfCapAttempt(m, s):
    p = (1 + Phi(-m / s)) / 2
    RETURN 1 + m + s * PhiInv(p)

FUNCTION HalfAttemptsForTarget(kTarget, s):
    // Solve m = kTarget - 1 - s * PhiInv((1 + Phi(-m/s)) / 2).
    m = kTarget - 1
    REPEAT 20 times:
        m = kTarget - 1 - s * PhiInv((1 + Phi(-m / s)) / 2)
    RETURN m

FUNCTION NominalBackoff(k):
    REQUIRE k >= 1
    A  = Dcap - D0
    p0 = Phi(-m / s)
    p  = Phi(((k - 1) - m) / s)
    f  = (p - p0) / (1 - p0)
    RETURN clamp(D0 + A * f, D0, Dcap)

FUNCTION BackoffDelay(k, rng):
    d = NominalBackoff(k)
    IF j > 0:
        u = rng.uniform01()
        d = d * (1 + j * (2 * u - 1))
    RETURN round(clamp(d, D0, Dcap))

FUNCTION Window(k):
    z = (k - muw) / sw
    RETURN Aw * exp(-0.5 * z * z)

FUNCTION UnitWindow(k):
    z = (k - muw) / sw
    RETURN exp(-0.5 * z * z)

FUNCTION ComposedDelay(k, rng):
    // Canonical monotone composition: capped increment-multiplicative.
    d = D0
    prev = D0
    FOR i = 1 TO k:
        nominal = NominalBackoff(i)
        increment = nominal - prev
        d = d + increment * (1 + w * UnitWindow(i))
        prev = nominal
    d = min(Dcap, d)
    IF j > 0:
        u = rng.uniform01()
        d = d * (1 + j * (2 * u - 1))
    RETURN round(clamp(d, D0, Dcap))

FUNCTION AdditiveDelay(k, rng):
    // Alternative; monotone only when the condition in 5.5 holds.
    d = NominalBackoff(k) + Window(k)
    IF j > 0:
        u = rng.uniform01()
        d = d * (1 + j * (2 * u - 1))
    RETURN round(clamp(d, D0, Dcap))

PROCEDURE Retry(operation):
    FOR attempt = 0 TO maxAttempts - 1:
        TRY:
            RETURN operation()
        CATCH Cancellation:
            RETHROW
        CATCH error:
            IF attempt == maxAttempts - 1:
                RETHROW
            IF NOT isTransient(error):
                RETHROW
            k = attempt + 1
            delay = ComposedDelay(k, rng)
            SLEEP delay
```

### 7.3 Complexity

Both delay functions are $O(1)$ in time and space. The increment-multiplicative composition is $O(k)$ if implemented by summation; for large $k$, cache the cumulative sum or precompute the schedule. `HalfAttemptsForTarget` and `HalfCapAttempt` are configuration-time operations; the bisection `PhiInv` costs about 100 `Phi` evaluations and is not on the retry path.

## 8. C# reference implementation

The complete implementation is in `GaussianBackoff.cs`. The essential parts are reproduced below.

### 8.1 Normal CDF

```csharp
public static double Erf(double x)
{
    if (!double.IsFinite(x))
        throw new ArgumentOutOfRangeException(nameof(x), "x must be finite.");

    double sign = Math.Sign(x);
    x = Math.Abs(x);

    const double p  = 0.3275911;
    const double a1 = 0.254829592;
    const double a2 = -0.284496736;
    const double a3 = 1.421413741;
    const double a4 = -1.453152027;
    const double a5 = 1.061405429;

    double t = 1.0 / (1.0 + p * x);
    double y = 1.0 - (((((a5 * t + a4) * t + a3) * t + a2) * t + a1) * t)
               * Math.Exp(-x * x);
    return sign * y;
}

public static double Phi(double z)
    => 0.5 * (1.0 + Erf(z / Math.Sqrt(2.0)));
```

The `erf` approximation is Abramowitz & Stegun 7.1.26, with a maximum absolute error of about $1.5 \times  10^{-7}$ — far below millisecond resolution.

### 8.2 Configuration

```csharp
public sealed record BackoffOptions
{
    public int InitialMs { get; init; } = 500;
    public int CapMs { get; init; } = 30_000;
    public double HalfAttempts { get; init; } = 3.0;      // m
    public double RampWidthAttempts { get; init; } = 6.0; // W; sigma = W / 4
    public double JitterFraction { get; init; } = 0.0;    // j

    internal double Sigma => RampWidthAttempts / 4.0;
}
```

### 8.3 Nominal and jittered delay

```csharp
public static double NominalDelayMs(int k, BackoffOptions o)
{
    if (k < 1)
        throw new ArgumentOutOfRangeException(nameof(k));

    double amplitude = o.CapMs - o.InitialMs;
    double s = o.Sigma;
    double m = o.HalfAttempts;
    double n = k - 1;

    double p0 = GaussianMath.Phi(-m / s);
    double p  = GaussianMath.Phi((n - m) / s);
    double fraction = (p - p0) / (1.0 - p0);

    return Math.Clamp(o.InitialMs + amplitude * fraction,
                      o.InitialMs, o.CapMs);
}

public static int DelayMs(int k, BackoffOptions o, Random? random = null)
{
    double delay = NominalDelayMs(k, o);
    return RetryJitter.DelayMs(
        delay, o.JitterFraction, o.InitialMs, o.CapMs, random);
}
```

`RetryJitter` owns the $j > 0$ branch, the clamp, and the single rounding step. `Random.Shared` is thread-safe and seeded from process entropy. It is intentionally **not** seeded from the request, so jitter is not reproducible per request. In tests, inject a seeded `Random` to make assertions deterministic.

### 8.4 Window

```csharp
public static double WindowMs(int k, WindowOptions o)
{
    if (k < 1)
        throw new ArgumentOutOfRangeException(nameof(k));

    o.Validate();
    double z = (k - o.CenterAttempt) / o.WidthAttempts;
    return o.PeakMs * Math.Exp(-0.5 * z * z);
}
```

### 8.5 Retry loop

```csharp
// retryAfterMs: optional Func<Exception, int?> supplying a server-directed delay.
for (int attempt = 0; attempt < maxAttempts; attempt++)
{
    try
    {
        return await operation(cancellationToken).ConfigureAwait(false);
    }
    catch (OperationCanceledException)
    {
        throw;
    }
    catch (Exception ex) when (attempt < maxAttempts - 1
                               && (shouldRetry?.Invoke(ex) ?? true))
    {
        int retryIndex = attempt + 1; // k = 1 for the first retry
        int localDelay = GaussianBackoff.DelayMs(retryIndex, options);
        int delay = RetryPolicy.EffectiveDelayMs(localDelay, retryAfterMs?.Invoke(ex));
        onRetry?.Invoke(retryIndex, delay);
        await Task.Delay(delay, cancellationToken).ConfigureAwait(false);
    }
}
```

Here `maxAttempts` counts the initial attempt. With `maxAttempts = 6`, there are five retries, delayed by $D(1)$ through $D(5)$. The optional `retryAfterMs` callback supplies any server-directed delay; `RetryPolicy.EffectiveDelayMs` takes the maximum, so the wait may exceed $D_{\mathrm{cap}}$.

### 8.6 Exact half-cap helpers

```csharp
public static double PhiInv(double p)
{
    if (!double.IsFinite(p) || p <= 0.0 || p >= 1.0)
        throw new ArgumentOutOfRangeException(nameof(p), "p must lie in (0, 1).");

    double lo = -40.0, hi = 40.0;
    for (int i = 0; i < 100; i++)
    {
        double mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi);
        if (Phi(mid) < p) lo = mid; else hi = mid;
    }
    return 0.5 * (lo + hi);
}

public static double HalfCapAttempt(BackoffOptions o)
{
    o.Validate();
    double m = o.HalfAttempts;
    double s = o.Sigma;
    double p = 0.5 * (1.0 + GaussianMath.Phi(-m / s));
    return 1.0 + m + s * GaussianMath.PhiInv(p);
}

public static double HalfAttemptsForTarget(double targetAttempt, double rampWidthAttempts)
{
    if (targetAttempt <= 1.0)
        throw new ArgumentOutOfRangeException(nameof(targetAttempt), "Target must exceed 1.");
    if (rampWidthAttempts <= 0.0)
        throw new ArgumentOutOfRangeException(nameof(rampWidthAttempts), "Ramp width must be positive.");

    double s = rampWidthAttempts / 4.0;
    double m = targetAttempt - 1.0;
    for (int i = 0; i < 20; i++)
        m = targetAttempt - 1.0 - s * GaussianMath.PhiInv(0.5 * (1.0 + GaussianMath.Phi(-m / s)));

    if (m < 0.0)
        throw new ArgumentOutOfRangeException(
            nameof(targetAttempt), "Target is unreachable with a non-negative HalfAttempts.");

    return m;
}
```

`HalfCapAttempt` returns the exact $k^*$ of §4.3. `HalfAttemptsForTarget` inverts it by fixed-point iteration, so $m = K_{\mathrm{half}} - 1$ is only the starting guess.

### 8.7 Canonical increment-multiplicative composition

```csharp
private static double IncrementWindowDelay(
    int k, BackoffOptions backoff, WindowOptions window, double w)
{
    double delay = backoff.InitialMs;
    double previousNominal = backoff.InitialMs;

    for (int j = 1; j <= k; j++)
    {
        double nominal = GaussianBackoff.NominalDelayMs(j, backoff);
        double increment = nominal - previousNominal;
        double g = window.PeakMs <= 0
            ? 0.0
            : GaussianWindow.WindowMs(j, window) / window.PeakMs;
        delay += increment * (1.0 + w * g);
        previousNominal = nominal;
    }

    return Math.Min(backoff.CapMs, delay);
}
```

The `Math.Min` is the cap required by S5.4; without it the accumulated window can exceed `CapMs`.

### 8.8 Layer separation and precomputed schedules

The formulas above are correct but mix concerns. A production implementation should expose five separate layers:

| Layer                 | Responsibility                                                                     |
| --------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `GaussianBackoff`     | Nominal monotone delay from the normalised CDF (S4)                                |
| `GaussianWindow`      | Optional Gaussian window value (S5)                                                |
| `RetryComposition`    | Additive, multiplicative, or capped increment-multiplicative rule (S5.4)           |
| `RetryJitter`         | Symmetric jitter, clamping, and integer quantization (S4.7–S4.8)                   |
| `RetryPolicy`         | Attempt limits, elapsed-time budget, `Retry-After`, and $D_{\mathrm{eff}}$ (S10.5) |
| `PrecomputedSchedule` | Cached cumulative curve for the increment-multiplicative form                      |

Keeping these separate prevents a specific error: a caller can accidentally treat a non-monotone additive composition as if it inherited the monotonicity guarantee of the base CDF. Only the base CDF and the capped increment-multiplicative composition carry that guarantee.

**Cumulative and precomputed schedules.** The increment-multiplicative form is $O(k)$ when evaluated by summation. Because the schedule depends only on the configuration, not on the operation, the usual fix is to precompute the nominal curve once per configuration:

```csharp
public sealed class PrecomputedSchedule
{
    private readonly double[] _delays;   // index 0 unused; k = 1..capacity

    public PrecomputedSchedule(
        BackoffOptions backoff, WindowOptions? window, double w, int capacity)
    {
        backoff.Validate();
        capacity = Math.Max(1, capacity);
        _delays = new double[capacity + 1];

        double delay = backoff.InitialMs;
        double previousNominal = backoff.InitialMs;
        _delays[1] = delay;

        for (int k = 2; k <= capacity; k++)
        {
            double nominal = GaussianBackoff.NominalDelayMs(k, backoff);
            double increment = nominal - previousNominal;
            double g = window is null || window.PeakMs <= 0
                ? 0.0
                : GaussianWindow.WindowMs(k, window) / window.PeakMs;

            delay += increment * (1.0 + w * g);
            _delays[k] = Math.Min(backoff.CapMs, delay);
            previousNominal = nominal;
        }
    }

    public double NominalDelayMs(int k) =>
        k >= 1 && k < _delays.Length
            ? _delays[k]
            : throw new ArgumentOutOfRangeException(nameof(k));
}
```

The cumulative form is the same computation expressed directly:

$$
\begin{aligned}
C(k) &= C(k-1) + \Delta D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(k)\,(1 + w\,G(k)), \qquad C(1) = D_0\\
D(k) &= \min(D_{\mathrm{cap}}, C(k))
\end{aligned}
$$

Precomputation is appropriate when many operations share one configuration. It is not appropriate when the configuration is per-request, and it must be invalidated when the configuration changes. The array is bounded by `maxAttempts`, so memory is negligible.

## 10. Gotchas and operational notes

### 10.1 Semantics and off-by-one errors

1. **Define the indexing convention once.** This specification uses $k = 1$ for the first retry after the initial failure. If a caller passes `attempt = 0` for the initial attempt, do not call $D(0)$. Either return zero delay for the initial attempt or start the retry index at 1.
2. **`maxAttempts` counts attempts, not retries.** With `maxAttempts = 6`, there are five retries. Document this in the API; it is a common source of confusion.
3. **The first retry has a non-zero delay, in the modes that guarantee it.** $D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(1) = D_0$ and the capped increment-multiplicative composition preserve $D(1) = D_0$. Additive, multiplicative, and standalone-window modes do not (S5.4). If the product requires an immediate first retry, special-case $k = 1$ outside the schedule.

### 10.2 Numerical issues

4. **Validate the parameter ranges before evaluating the CDF.** Require $D_{\mathrm{cap}} > D_0 \ge  0$, $m \ge  0$, $s > 0$, $0 \le  j < 1$, $A_w \ge  0$, $\sigma_w > 0$, $w \ge  0$, and `maxAttempts ≥ 1`. Reject `NaN` and infinities explicitly: `double.IsFinite(...)` in .NET, an equivalent check elsewhere. A `NaN` parameter silently poisons every comparison, and the usual `clamp` implementations propagate it rather than catching it. Validate once at configuration time, not on the retry path.
5. **The normalization denominator is bounded below.** $1 - \Phi (-m/s) \ge  0.5$ for $m \ge  0$, so the canonical form is numerically stable. Two caveats remain. First, the numerator is a difference of two CDF values and can lose relative precision for very large $m/s$; if the parameter range is widened well beyond the profiles in S6, use a library `erfc`-based implementation rather than the A&S `erf` approximation. Second, the simplified asymptotic form is even simpler but does not start exactly at $D_0$. Do not treat $m = K_{\mathrm{half}} - 1$ as exact; the true half-cap is $m + 1 + \delta$ (S4.3), and `HalfAttemptsForTarget` solves for the exact value.
6. **Handle $j = 0$ explicitly.** The clamped-distribution formulas in S4.8 divide by $H - L$, which is zero when $j = 0$. Branch on $j > 0$ and return the deterministic delay otherwise. Do not let a $0/0$ reach the scheduler.
7. **Decide the sub-millisecond policy.** If $D_0 \ge  1$, every rounded delay is at least 1 ms. If $D_0 = 0$ is allowed, choose between forbidding it, allowing `0` as "immediate", or flooring at 1 ms, and document the choice (S4.7).
8. **Round only once.** Compute the curve in `double`, apply jitter, clamp, and round at the end. Rounding intermediate values accumulates error and can distort the ramp.
9. **Watch integer range.** `int` milliseconds overflow beyond about 24.8 days. For long caps, use `long` or `TimeSpan`. `Task.Delay` also has practical limits.
10. **The `erf` approximation has finite accuracy.** The A&S 7.1.26 error ($\approx  1.5 \times  10^{-7}$) is negligible at millisecond resolution. If you need more accuracy, use a library implementation or a higher-order approximation.

### 10.3 Jitter

11. **Jitter breaks strict monotonicity.** $D_j(k + 1)$ can be less than $D_j(k)$ even when the nominal schedule is monotone. Decide whether this matters. If it does, use positive-only jitter, increment jitter, or disable jitter.
12. **Do not seed jitter from the request ID.** The requirement is non-reproducible jitter. Use a thread-safe entropy source such as `Random.Shared` or a CSPRNG. Seeding from a request identifier makes jitter predictable and can reintroduce synchronization.
13. **Do not create a new `Random` per call.** On older runtimes, `new Random()` can repeat seeds and produce identical streams. Use `Random.Shared` (thread-safe in .NET 6+) or a per-thread instance.
14. **Clamp after jitter, and know what clamping does.** A positive multiplier can push the delay above the cap; a negative one can push it below the floor. Clamp the final value. The clamp is not a cosmetic detail: at $k = 1$ half of the symmetric jitter mass clips at $D_0$. At the cap the half-mass clipping applies when the nominal input is **exactly** $D_{\mathrm{cap}}$ (after explicit saturation or rounding), not automatically at every late retry (S4.8).
15. **Jitter is not a security control.** It reduces correlated retries; it does not prevent abuse.

### 10.4 Windows and composition

16. **Additive and multiplicative windows can create a dip.** The composed schedule is no longer monotone if the window’s negative slope exceeds the backoff’s positive slope. Use the conditions in S5.5, keep the window wide and low, or use the capped increment-multiplicative form.
17. **Cap the increment-multiplicative sum explicitly.** The uncapped sum can exceed $D_{\mathrm{cap}}$; $\min(D_{\mathrm{cap}}, \cdot )$ is part of the definition, not an optional safeguard (S5.4).
18. **Do not stack windows by simple addition.** Two overlapping windows can exceed the cap or create unintended peaks. Use `max` of the windows, normalize, or design a single window.
19. **A standalone window is not a backoff.** It intentionally speeds up after the peak, and it does not preserve $D(1) = D_0$. Use it only when both behaviours are desired.
20. **The window affects the cap.** Additive windows can saturate the cap earlier than the backoff alone. Check the composed sequence, not just the backoff.

### 10.5 Distributed systems

21. **Jitter must be per client, not global.** A shared jitter value synchronizes clients instead of desynchronizing them.
22. **Honor `Retry-After`, and keep the two caps separate.** If the server sends `Retry-After`, the effective wait is $D_{\mathrm{eff}} = \max(D_{\mathrm{local}}, \mathrm{retryAfter})$. Server guidance overrides the client heuristic. The local scheduling cap bounds $D_{\mathrm{local}}$, not $D_{\mathrm{eff}}$: a client cap of 30 s combined with `Retry-After: 120` yields a 120 s wait. Document that distinction, and apply any absolute operation deadline separately. If a hard ceiling really is required, it must be a separate policy setting, not the scheduling cap.
23. **Retries need a budget, and the wait must be cancellable.** Cap total attempts and total elapsed time. A backoff alone does not prevent an infinite retry loop. Pass the cancellation token to the delay (`Task.Delay(delay, token)`) so shutdown and user cancellation are not blocked by a long cap.
24. **Retry only transient failures.** Do not retry validation errors, authentication failures, or non-idempotent operations unless the operation is explicitly idempotent.
25. **Use a circuit breaker.** When the breaker is open, fail fast instead of sleeping. The backoff is for the half-open/probing state and for transient errors.
26. **Measure with a monotonic clock.** Wall-clock changes (NTP adjustments, daylight saving) should not affect elapsed-time budgets. `Task.Delay` uses a monotonic timer, but application-level budget calculations should too.
27. **Reset the attempt counter after a quiet period.** If a user retries manually hours later, starting at $k = 1$ is usually correct; decide explicitly whether the counter is per operation, per session, or per time window.

### 10.6 Product and security

28. **Backoff is not rate limiting.** A malicious client can ignore it. Enforce limits server-side.
29. **Backoff is not a substitute for idempotency.** Retrying a non-idempotent operation can duplicate side effects.
30. **Long delays can look like a hang.** For user-facing operations, show progress or a “retrying…” state, and keep the cap modest.
31. **Predictable schedules can be gamed.** For abuse-sensitive flows, combine the schedule with server-side quotas and risk scoring.

### 10.7 Testing

32. **Separate nominal and jittered functions.** Test the deterministic curve exhaustively; test jitter statistically.
33. **Inject randomness in tests.** Pass a seeded `Random` to make jittered tests reproducible.
34. **Assert the invariants.** $D(1) = D_0$ for the base CDF and the capped increment form, $D(k) \le  D_{\mathrm{cap}}$, $D(k + 1) \ge  D(k)$ for the nominal curve, composed monotonicity for the capped increment form, and bounds (not monotonicity) for realized jittered delays.
35. **Test the composition.** Additive and multiplicative windows can break monotonicity; assert the intended behavior explicitly, including the cap and the first retry.

## 11. Validation and testing

### 11.1 Invariants

For every valid configuration and every $k \ge  1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{Nominal base CDF:}\\
&\qquad D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(1) = D_0,\quad
D_0 \le D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(k) < D_{\mathrm{cap}},\quad
D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(k+1) \ge D_{\mathrm{backoff}}(k)\\[0.4em]
&\text{Composed nominal (capped increment-multiplicative):}\\
&\qquad D_c(1) = D_0,\quad
D_0 \le D_c(k) \le D_{\mathrm{cap}},\quad
D_c(k+1) \ge D_c(k)\\[0.4em]
&\text{Composed nominal (additive / multiplicative / standalone):}\\
&\qquad D(1)\ \text{may exceed}\ D_0,\quad
D_0 \le D(k) \le D_{\mathrm{cap}}\\
&\qquad \text{monotonicity only if the section 5.5 condition holds for the chosen horizon } K\\[0.4em]
&\text{Jitter (before clamping):}\\
&\qquad |D_j(k) - D(k)| \le j\,D(k)\quad \text{when } j > 0\\
&\qquad D_j(k) = D(k)\quad \text{when } j = 0\\[0.4em]
&\text{Realized:}\\
&\qquad D_0 \le D_j(k) \le D_{\mathrm{cap}}\quad
\text{(local bound; } D_{\mathrm{eff}} \text{ with Retry-After may exceed } D_{\mathrm{cap}}\text{)}\\
&\qquad \text{(monotonicity is not guaranteed)}
\end{aligned}
$$

The three levels are defined in S4.9. A test or document that claims “the delay is monotone” must say which level it means, and a claim about the first retry must name the composition.

### 11.2 Property-based tests

Generate random valid configurations and assert the invariants over $k = 1 \ldots  1{,}000$. This catches edge cases such as very small $s$, very large $m$, and caps close to $D_0$.

### 11.3 Golden tests

Pin a small set of parameter combinations to expected sequences, as in S9. Golden tests catch accidental changes to the formula or the rounding convention.

## 12. Comparison with exponential backoff

| Property      | Gaussian CDF backoff                                                | Exponential backoff                          |
| ------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Formula       | $D_0 + A\cdot \Phi ((n-m)/s)$ normalized                            | $\min(D_{\mathrm{cap}}, D_0\cdot r^{k-1})$   |
| Monotone      | Yes                                                                 | Yes                                          |
| Bounded       | Yes, by construction                                                | Yes, after `min`                             |
| Initial delay | Exact $D_0$ in the base CDF; mode-specific after composition (§5.4) | Exact $D_0$                                  |
| Knee          | Smooth, tunable                                                     | Abrupt at the cap                            |
| Parameters    | $D_0$, $D_{\mathrm{cap}}$, $m$, $s$                                 | $D_0$, $D_{\mathrm{cap}}$, growth factor $r$ |
| Growth        | Gradual, S-shaped                                                   | Aggressive, geometric                        |
| Standard      | Less common                                                         | Industry default                             |

Use the Gaussian CDF when you want a gentle, predictable ramp and a soft shoulder. Use exponential backoff when you want aggressive early growth and the simplest possible implementation. Both should be paired with jitter.

## 13. Related work

This section places the two branches against existing practice. The literature is large, so the aim is narrow: identify the nearest neighbours, state what is shared, and make the differences explicit. Numbered citations refer to S15.4.

### 13.1 Exponential backoff and jitter

The dominant retry policy is exponential backoff, $D(k) = \min(D_{\mathrm{cap}}, D_0 \cdot  r^{k-1})$, usually with $r = 2$ [3], [4]. Its lineage runs through Ethernet's binary exponential backoff [1] and TCP's retransmission timer [5]. Brooker's widely cited treatment adds jitter and compares full, equal, and decorrelated variants, showing that decorrelated jitter spreads a client population best but widens the delay distribution [3].

**Shared.** Attempt-indexed delay, a cap, optional jitter, and the same purpose: desynchronise clients that failed together.

**Different.** Exponential backoff is piecewise geometric, with a discontinuous derivative at every attempt and an abrupt knee at the cap. The CDF schedule is a smooth, strictly increasing curve with a tunable knee, and in its base form its first retry is exactly $D_0$ by construction. Brooker's variants randomise either the delay level or the increment; this specification uses symmetric multiplicative jitter, treats it as optional, and requires that it not be reproducible per request.

### 13.2 Binary exponential backoff and contention windows

Ethernet's truncated binary exponential backoff selects a random slot from $[0, 2^{\min(n,10)} - 1]$ after the $n$-th collision [1]. IEEE 802.11 DCF applies the same idea with a contention window bounded by `CW_min` and `CW_max` [2]. The delay is therefore a random draw whose *support* grows with the attempt count.

**Shared.** A cap, truncation, per-attempt growth, and the insight that a retry population must be spread out in time.

**Different.** Randomness is inside the policy rather than added on top of it. The present schedule is deterministic before jitter, and the window is a deterministic hump rather than a sampling range. Neither BEB nor DCF contains a CDF-shaped delay curve.

### 13.3 Protocol timer ladders and server-directed delays

Several protocols specify hand-tuned ladders rather than formulas. TCP doubles its retransmission timeout on each retransmission up to a maximum [5]. SIP retransmits at `T1, 2T1, 4T1, …`, capped at `T2` (defaults 500 ms and 4 s) [6]. HTTP servers can direct client pacing with `Retry-After` [7].

**Shared.** A bounded increasing sequence with a specified first delay.

**Different.** These are tables, not parameterised curves, and they are not designed to be retuned per workload. `Retry-After` is authoritative and orthogonal: the correct behaviour is $\max(D_{\mathrm{local}}, \mathrm{retryAfter})$, not a blend. This means the local scheduling cap does not bound the effective wait; a server can request a longer delay than the client's cap (§1, §10.5). This paper treats server guidance as an override and keeps $D_{\mathrm{cap}}$ and $D_{\mathrm{eff}}$ distinct.

### 13.4 Retry policies in libraries and service meshes

Mainstream implementations expose exponential backoff with jitter. Polly and `Polly.Contrib.WaitAndRetry` provide constant, linear, exponential, and decorrelated-jitter strategies [8]. Resilience4j offers a configurable exponential retry with jitter [9]. Envoy and gRPC express retry policy as `maxAttempts`, a backoff base, and a maximum interval [10], [11]. AWS SDKs use a “standard” retry mode with exponential backoff and a token-bucket retry quota [12].

**Shared.** The API shape: initial delay, cap, growth, jitter, and an attempt budget.

**Different.** None of these exposes a smooth analytic curve or a separate window function. They confirm that the engineering envelope used here — cap, attempt budget, jitter, idempotency — is the right one, while leaving the shape of the curve as an open design choice.

### 13.5 Gaussian and alternative backoff distributions in wireless MAC

The phrase “Gaussian backoff” already has a meaning in multiple-access research: it describes the *distribution* from which a contention window or backoff counter is drawn, not a deterministic attempt-indexed delay. Rivero-Angeles et al. derive Gaussian approximations for the access-delay distribution under several backoff policies in S-ALOHA [13]. Sun and Dai compare quadratic backoff with BEB in 802.11 DCF [14]. Misra and Khatua propose a semi-distributed scheme that migrates from random to deterministic backoff as collisions are resolved [15]. Nilsson and Jäntti generalise the analysis to arbitrary stationary backoff distributions [16]. Staden evaluates a “CSMA normalized Gaussian backoff time” alongside other distributions [17].

**Shared.** The Gaussian family, a bounded backoff, and the goal of reducing correlated collisions.

**Different.** In all of these, randomness is the mechanism and the Gaussian is either a sampling distribution or an analytical approximation. Here the Gaussian CDF is a deterministic map from attempt index to delay, and the PDF is a deterministic bump in the same index. In the reviewed MAC literature we found no analogue of the endpoint-normalised curve or of monotone increment composition.

### 13.6 Mathematical relatives

Three standard constructions produce the same shape:

- The **normal CDF** is the integral of the Gaussian and the basis of the **probit** transform $\Phi^{-1}$; a probit-transformed linear ramp is exactly the curve of S4.2 after reparameterisation [18].
- The **logistic (sigmoid) curve** is a close relative with a cheaper closed form and slightly heavier tails. A logistic backoff would behave almost identically; no widely used retry library adopts one.
- **Smoothstep / smootherstep** easing functions perform the same normalised interpolation between two endpoints with flattened ends, and are standard in computer graphics rather than retry scheduling [19].

The Gaussian window of S5 is the kernel used as a **Gabor/Morlet window** in signal processing, where localisation in a transform domain is the entire point.

**Shared.** The mathematics is classical. Nothing in S3–S5 requires new analysis.

**Different.** The contribution is the mapping of these objects onto retry semantics — CDF as delay, PDF as rate, window as a deliberate cooldown band — together with the engineering constraints around them.

### 13.7 Retry windows, probing, and progressive retry

A non-monotone delay schedule is unusual in retry theory, which normally assumes that delay should only grow. The nearest operational analogues are:

- **Circuit-breaker half-open probing**, where a limited number of probes is admitted and their spacing is a policy choice rather than a backoff.
- **Progressive retry**, which escalates recovery actions across attempts instead of only lengthening the wait [20].
- Work on **retry amplification**, which shows that uncoordinated retries can turn a partial outage into a cascading failure and argues for budgets and coordination rather than stronger backoff alone [21].

**Shared.** The idea that the distribution of attempts over time matters, not just the total wait.

**Different.** None of these proposes a smooth, parameterised hump centred on a chosen attempt, nor a composition rule that combines a hump with a monotone schedule while preserving monotonicity and the cap. Within the literature reviewed for this specification we did not identify the capped increment-multiplicative composition; that is an observation about the search, not a priority claim.

### 13.8 How this paper differs

Taken together, the reviewed work covers each ingredient separately; within that literature we did not find them combined in a single scheduling construction:

1. **Deterministic Gaussian-CDF delay.** Exponential and BEB policies are geometric or random; timer ladders are tabular. A normalised CDF with an exact first-retry delay and a tunable knee does not appear to be a standard retry policy.
2. **PDF as the growth rate.** The MAC literature uses the Gaussian as a sampling distribution or an approximation. Reading the density as the derivative of the delay is what connects the original hump formula to a monotone schedule.
3. **A separate, composable retry window.** The hump is retained as a first-class object with standalone and composed forms, rather than being discarded or replaced by jitter.
4. **Monotone composition with a hump.** The capped increment-multiplicative rule lets a non-monotone window modify a monotone schedule without breaking monotonicity or the cap.
5. **A single engineering envelope.** One-based indexing, integer-millisecond output, an implementor-chosen cap, non-reproducible jitter, and an operational checklist are specified together.

What is *not* claimed as new: the normal CDF, the probit transform, the Gaussian kernel, sigmoid easing, or jittered backoff in general. The following is a statement about the search, not a proof of novelty:

> Within the literature reviewed for this specification, we did not identify an equivalent retry-scheduling construction combining an endpoint-normalised Gaussian CDF delay, a separately parameterised Gaussian retry window, and capped increment-multiplicative composition. This is a preliminary observation rather than a claim of mathematical priority.

Searches of Crossref and Semantic Scholar did not surface such a combination, but absence of evidence is not proof of novelty, and the wireless-MAC usage of “Gaussian backoff” means the name alone is not distinctive. A completed novelty review would need a systematic search across retry libraries, patents, and the queueing literature, which this paper does not claim to provide.

## 14. Future work and empirical validation

### 14.1 Asymmetric and skew-normal windows

The Gaussian window is symmetric around $\mu_w$. Real recovery need not be: a service may degrade quickly and recover slowly, or the reverse. A skew-normal or two-piece normal window — with separate left and right widths $\sigma_{\mathrm{left}}$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{right}}$, joined continuously at $\mu_w$ — can express that asymmetry without abandoning the Gaussian family [23].

This is deliberately left out of the core model. It adds two parameters instead of one, complicates the monotonicity conditions of S5.5 (the binding side is the larger width), and has no evidence yet that it improves retry outcomes. It should be adopted only after simulation or measurement shows a benefit over the symmetric window.

### 14.2 Empirical validation

A reproducible synthetic comparison is included as `RetrySimulation.cs`, with its output in `retry-simulation-results.csv`. It compares six schedules under identical conditions:

- capped exponential backoff, no jitter;
- exponential with full jitter;
- exponential with equal jitter;
- exponential with decorrelated jitter;
- probit backoff, no jitter;
- probit backoff with symmetric jitter ($j = 0.2$).

**Model.** `2,000` clients fail simultaneously at $t = 0$. Service health is `0` until `2 s`, then rises linearly to `1` by `12 s`. Each attempt succeeds with probability equal to the current health; a failure schedules the next attempt with the strategy’s delay. The attempt budget is `8`, the local cap is `30 s`, and the horizon is `180 s`. All strategies use the same deterministic success draws (common random numbers keyed by client and attempt), so differences come from scheduling. The simulation deliberately has no admission control or queueing: it isolates the retry schedule from server capacity.

| Strategy                           | Success | p50 (ms) | p95 (ms) | p99 (ms) | Peak retry arrivals / 100 ms | Attempts |
| ---------------------------------- | ------- | -------- | -------- | -------- | ---------------------------- | -------- |
| Exponential, no jitter             | 100.0%  | 7,500    | 15,500   | 15,500   | 2,000                        | 10,506   |
| Exponential, full jitter           | 99.8%   | 9,716    | 21,778   | 28,893   | 597                          | 12,432   |
| Exponential, equal jitter          | 100.0%  | 10,380   | 18,806   | 24,936   | 824                          | 11,197   |
| Exponential, decorrelated jitter   | 98.8%   | 7,934    | 16,658   | 20,599   | 270                          | 10,037   |
| Probit, no jitter                  | 100.0%  | 10,503   | 25,410   | 25,410   | 2,000                        | 8,082    |
| Probit, symmetric jitter ($j=0.2$) | 100.0%  | 10,652   | 25,700   | 27,744   | 1,019                        | 8,090    |

**What the results show.**

- **Curve shape does not fix synchronization.** Both deterministic schedules produce a `2,000`-retry burst exactly `500 ms` after the outage. The peak is identical because both start every client at the same delay. A monotone curve, however smooth, cannot desynchronize a fleet on its own.
- **Jitter is the dominant control on the retry spike.** Peak retry arrivals fall from `2,000` to `597` (full jitter), `824` (equal jitter), `270` (decorrelated), or `1,019` (symmetric jitter on probit). The cost appears in tail latency and, for the most aggressive strategies, in attempt exhaustion.
- **Probit uses fewer attempts here.** `8,082` total attempts versus `10,506` for exponential, because its gentler post-knee ramp spreads later retries. In this profile that efficiency comes with a higher `p50` and `p95`; the schedule is slower to recover for the median client.
- **No strategy dominates.** Decorrelated jitter has the lowest peak but the highest attempt exhaustion and a wide latency distribution. Probit has the fewest attempts but the slowest tails. Full jitter minimizes the spike but increases `p99`. The appropriate choice depends on whether the priority is aggregate stability, tail latency, or attempt budget.

These results do **not** show that probit backoff is operationally superior. They support a narrower claim: the nominal curve and the jitter policy are separate decisions, and the curve shape alone does not address synchronized retries.

**Limitations.** One synthetic health profile, one deterministic seed set, no admission control, and no network or queueing effects. A publishable comparison needs multiple failure/recovery profiles, multiple seeds with confidence intervals, and real workload traces. The harness is included so those runs are reproducible.

### 15.1 Error-function approximation

The reference implementation uses Abramowitz & Stegun 7.1.26:

\mathrm{erf}(x) \approx 1 - \left(a_1 t + a_2 t^2 + a_3 t^3 + a_4 t^4 + a_5 t^5\right)e^{-x^2}

$$
\begin{aligned}

### 15.3 Glossary

- **Backoff:** a delay schedule that grows with the failure count.
- **Cap:** the local scheduling cap $D_{\mathrm{cap}}$, the maximum delay the algorithm generates. The effective retry delay $D_{\mathrm{eff}}$ after `Retry-After` may be larger.
- **CDF:** cumulative distribution function; the integral of the PDF.
- **Jitter:** random variation added to a delay.
- **PDF:** probability density function; the derivative of the CDF.
- **Probit:** the inverse of the standard normal CDF, $\Phi^{-1}$; a probit transform maps a probability to a real-valued score.
- **Probit backoff:** the recommended name for Branch A, because the schedule applies the normal CDF — whose inverse is the probit — to a linear ramp in the attempt index.
- **Retry window:** a localized hump-shaped delay contribution centered on a chosen attempt.
- **Saturation:** the plateau a bounded curve approaches as its input grows.
