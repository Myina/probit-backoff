# Gaussian Retry Backoff and Retry-Window Functions

Gaussian Retry Backoff and Retry-Window Functions presents a mathematical and implementation specification for an alternative retry-scheduling approach. Its central contribution is a bounded, attempt-indexed delay curve derived from the Gaussian cumulative distribution function (CDF), alongside a separate Gaussian probability-density function (PDF) window for adding delay around selected retry attempts.

A Gaussian PDF produces a hump-shaped curve that rises to a peak and then falls. Used directly as a backoff schedule, it can make later retries wait less than earlier ones. Instead, the paper uses the integral of the Gaussian density, represented by the normal CDF, as the delay function. This creates a smooth, non-decreasing curve that begins at a configured initial delay and approaches a configurable maximum. Parameters control the location and width of the transition. The paper derives the exact half-cap retry attempt.

The second function is a localized Gaussian retry window. It creates additional delay around a chosen attempt and then tapers away. It can operate independently or be combined with the monotone backoff. The paper examines additive, multiplicative, and increment-multiplicative composition. It identifies capped increment-multiplicative composition as the preferred method when the combined nominal schedule must remain non-decreasing. This method applies the window to increments of the base backoff, rather than to the accumulated delay, and explicitly caps the result.

The specification distinguishes the guarantees offered by each mode. The exact first-retry delay is preserved by the base CDF and capped increment-multiplicative composition, but not generally by a standalone window or additive and multiplicative composition. Jitter is a separate, optional stage. It helps reduce synchronized retry traffic, but independent jitter means actual delays are not guaranteed to increase from one retry to the next. The paper explains how clamping changes the jitter distribution at the initial delay and cap.

The paper distinguishes the algorithm’s local scheduling cap from the effective wait after a server supplies a `Retry-After` instruction. Server-directed delays take precedence and may exceed the local cap. Other implementation guidance covers attempt indexing, integer rounding, parameter validation, numerical precision, cancellation, retry limits, elapsed-time budgets, and transient-failure handling.

Practical material includes language-agnostic pseudocode, a C# reference implementation, precomputed-schedule guidance, and worked scenarios involving API calls, background jobs, verification-code resends, database retries, and cooldown windows. The paper compares the proposed curve with exponential backoff and surveys related approaches in networking protocols, retry libraries, and service infrastructure. It recommends “probit backoff” as a clearer name for the CDF-based core model, retaining “Gaussian retry window” for the optional extension.

Finally, the paper reports implementation checks and a reproducible synthetic fleet simulation comparing six retry strategies across 2,000 clients during simulated service recovery. The results illustrate trade-offs in success rate, latency, retry volume, and synchronization. The conclusions are deliberately limited: the simulation excludes admission control and queueing, and does not include multi-seed confidence intervals or plots. The paper therefore presents a mathematically specified, implementable scheduling model, not proof of universal superiority over exponential backoff. Further validation under realistic service capacity, traffic, and recovery conditions is still required.
